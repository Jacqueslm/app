# Herbs: making your own to sell, or sourcing wholesale

Researched October 2026. This is research, not legal advice — the state-level
rules genuinely differ, so the last step of every section is "check your own
state."

---

## The short version

There are three ways in, and they are not equally good for a first year:

| Route | What it costs to start | Time to first sale | Margin | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Buy herbs wholesale, blend/package yourself** | $400–$900 | 2–6 weeks | 65–80% at retail | Almost everyone starting. No land, no certification, no waiting on a harvest. |
| **Grow and dry your own** | $1,500–$8,000 (land, seed, drying, irrigation) | 4–12 months | 80–90% at retail | Someone who already has land, water, and a season to spend. |
| **White-label / co-pack** | $3,000–$15,000 | 8–16 weeks | 30–50% | Someone with an audience or a shop already, who needs volume fast. |

The honest ordering is **wholesale first, grow second, co-pack last**. Wholesale
herbs let you find out whether anyone wants your tincture before you plant a
field or sign an MOQ for 500 bottles.

Realistic year-one gross sales for a one-person herbal business that shows up
every week: **$12,000–$36,000**. That is revenue. The USDA's (old) market survey
put median vendor sales at **$2,222 per market per year** — most people selling
at markets are not making real money, and the ones who are treat it like a job,
not a hobby.

## 1. What you're actually allowed to sell

Everything below turns on **the product type and the words you print**, not on
the fact that it contains herbs.

### The four categories that matter

| Product | Legal category | What that means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Bag of dried nettle leaf, sold as tea/culinary | **Conventional food** | Identity statement, net quantity, your business name and address. Nutrition Facts panel unless the small-business exemption applies. Easiest to sell; least claims you can make. |
| Tincture, capsule, powdered extract | **Dietary supplement** | Supplement Facts panel, GMP manufacturing, structure/function claims with the FDA disclaimer. This is where the real compliance weight sits. |
| Salve, balm, body oil | **Cosmetic** | No Supplement Facts. Cannot claim to treat eczema, infection, pain or anything else — that turns it into a drug. Safety substantiation and adverse-event obligations apply. |
| Anything sold as curing/treating a condition | **Drug** | Off the table without FDA approval. Don't. |

One important correction to a myth you will hear: **there is no small-business
exemption from the supplement GMP rules (21 CFR 111).** The "500 employees / 20
employees" numbers people quote are the 2008–2010 *phase-in dates* for when
compliance began, not thresholds that exempt a small maker. If you bottle
capsules or tinctures in your kitchen and sell them, Part 111 applies.

There *is* a separate small-business exemption from the **Nutrition Facts
panel** (roughly: ≤$500k annual sales, or ≤$50k in direct-to-consumer food
sales, plus a low-volume route), and separately a **$5,000 organic-sales**
exemption from organic *certification*. Neither one removes the GMP obligation.

### Claims: the exact line you cannot cross

Allowed, if true and substantiated: a **structure/function claim** — "supports
healthy digestion", "calcium builds strong bones".

Not allowed: anything naming or implying a disease. The regulated verbs are
**diagnose, treat, cure, prevent, mitigate**. So "helps you sleep" is fine;
"treats insomnia" is not. "Supports immune function" is fine; "prevents colds"
is not. Product names, images, and cited studies all count as claims.

Any structure/function claim needs the disclaimer, near the claim:

> This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration.
> This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

You must also notify FDA of a structure/function claim within **30 days after
first marketing**, and keep substantiation on file. A brand-new ingredient not
sold in the US before **15 Oct 1994** needs a New Dietary Ingredient
notification **75 days before** sale — worth checking before you build a
formula around something exotic.

### Where you can make it

- **Dried herbs and tea blends** are the friendliest: several states allow them
  under a cottage-food permit. Washington, for example: **$355 for a 2-year
  permit**, direct-to-consumer only, **$35,000/year cap**, dry tea and spice
  blends allowed, prepared beverages not.
- **Tinctures and capsules** are usually *not* cottage food. Minnesota says
  outright that dietary supplements are not allowed under its cottage-food
  exemption (its cap is $78,000); a supplement needs a food license and
  supplement rules.
- **Salves** are cosmetics: no cottage-food route, but also no kitchen license
  in most states. MoCRA adds safety substantiation and adverse-event duties;
  small businesses get some relief, but not for products intended for internal
  use.
- **A farmers market is not a loophole.** Market rules sit on top of state food
  law. Selling there doesn't make an unlicensed product legal.

So the practical split for a home-based start:

> **Start with dried herbs, tea blends, and salves. Add tinctures and capsules
> only after you have a compliant space and a label that survives review.**

### Registration, insurance, entity

- **FDA food facility registration:** a private residence is *not* a facility,
  and farms are exempt. But "farm" means a specific definition — growing, and
  drying/packaging *your own* raw crop. Buying herbs from Starwest and
  processing them is not a farm.
- **LLC vs sole prop:** an LLC costs $50–$500 depending on state and buys
  liability separation. It does not buy compliance, and it does not stop a claim
  for your own conduct.
- **Insurance:** general liability with product liability runs about
  **$45/month ($538/year)**; supplement-specific policies for small startups are
  quoted around **$1,500–$3,500/year**. Get this before your first sale, not
  after.
- **EIN** is free and direct from irs.gov. Ignore the sites charging for it.
- **TTB:** a botanical extract at **0.5% ABV or more** that's fit to drink may be
  regulated as an alcohol beverage — permits, tax, and alcohol labeling. Calling
  it a "tincture" does not settle it. This is the single most overlooked trap in
  alcohol-based tinctures.

## 3. What a unit actually costs you

Materials only — no labor, no insurance, no shipping.

**1 oz alcohol tincture** (1:5 ratio, ~5.7 g herb):

| | |
|---|---:|
| Herb | $0.34–$0.58 |
| Alcohol (25–40% ABV finished) | $0.18–$0.30 |
| Bottle + dropper | $1.22 (low qty) |
| Label | $0.20 |
| Carton | $0.25 |
| **Total** | **$2.19–$2.55** |

**2 oz calendula salve:** herb $0.25 + oil/wax $0.50–$0.90 + jar $0.48–$1.19 +
label $0.20 + carton $0.25 = **$1.68–$2.79**.

**100-count capsules** (500 mg each): powder ~$2.20 + capsules $1.80 + bottle
$0.30–$0.60 + label $0.20 + filler amortized $0.05 = **$4.54–$4.85**.

Note what dominates: **the bottle costs more than the herb**. For a tincture,
bottle + label + carton are about **two-thirds** of the materials cost; for a
100-count capsule bottle it is about a tenth, because the powder dominates there.
Buying bottles at 1,000 instead of 100 nearly halves that line — it is the
single cheapest improvement available to you.

## 4. What you can sell it for

Observed asking prices (small sample, not national averages — Etsy blocked
scraping and Amazon hid most prices, so treat these as bands to test):

| Product | Farmers market / local maker | Etsy listings | Defensible starting point |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 oz tincture | $13–$18 | $11–$18 | **$15–$18** |
| 2 oz salve | $10–$18 | $16–$30 | **$15–$22** |
| 4 oz loose tea | ~$10–$16 | ~$12.75 | **$12–$18** |
| 60-count capsules | — | $14–$36 (branded: $21–$32) | **$22–$32** |

### The margin math at both prices

| Product | Cost | Retail | Contribution | Wholesale (½ retail) | Contribution |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 oz tincture | $2.35 | $16 | **$13.65** | $8 | **$5.65** |
| 2 oz salve | $2.20 | $18 | **$15.80** | $9 | **$6.80** |
| 100 caps | $4.70 | $26 | **$21.30** | $13 | **$8.30** |

**Wholesale is not a discount — it's a different business.** At keystone (the
shop buys at half your retail and sells at your retail), your contribution per
unit drops by **about 60%** while your labor per unit stays exactly the same.

### What the channel takes

| Channel | Fees on a ~$16 sale |
|---|---|
| **Farmers market** | $10–$45 booth/day + your Saturday |
| **Etsy** | $0.20 listing + 6.5% transaction + 3% + $0.25 processing ≈ **$1.80 (11%)** |
| **Shopify** | $29/mo + 2.9% + $0.30 ≈ **$0.76 + platform** |
| **Amazon** | $39.99/mo (or $0.99/item) + 15% referral + FBA ~$3/unit + 3.5% logistics surcharge from Apr 2026; **supplements require third-party testing documentation** |
| **Faire (wholesale)** | 15% + $10 on a new customer's first order + 2.4% processing ≈ **24% of a $150 opening order** |

That Faire number matters: on a **$150 opening order** you pay about **$36.40 in
fees**, so a $75 product-cost order nets you roughly $38 — before shipping. If
your wholesale margin is already thin, Faire eats it.

### The two numbers that decide whether this is a business

1. **Break-even units.** A $2,000 startup (insurance, LLC, bottles, herbs,
   labels, booth) costs **~147 tinctures at retail** or **~354 at wholesale**.
2. **Effective hourly.** A market day is about 11 hours including prep and
   driving. At $250 in sales, the 85% contribution rate on a $16 tincture gives
   $213 gross profit; minus a $35 booth fee that is **$178 — about $16.20/hour**,
   before tax. Two market days a week at that rate is a part-time job, not a
   business. Getting to $500 days is what changes it, and that comes from repeat
   customers and bundles, not from more SKUs.

## 5. Grow it yourself, honestly

If you want to actually grow the herbs:

- **What's worth growing:** calendula, tulsi/holy basil, lemon balm, nettle,
  yarrow, chamomile, elderberry. All forgiving, all sell.
- **What isn't:** anything you can buy for $20/lb and that needs a dedicated
  drying room — ashwagandha, most roots. Your time is worth more than the $4 of
  herb in a bottle.
- **Drying is the bottleneck, not growing.** A small dehydrator handles a few
  pounds a week; a harvest glut rots in a day. Plan drying capacity before you
  plant area.
- **Organic certification** starts around **$625 + inspection** (Oregon Tilth:
  $300 application + $325 first-year). CCOF's application is ~$350. The USDA
  **cost-share refunds up to 75%, capped at $750 per scope**, applications due
  **31 Dec 2026** — but only if you're certified; under **$5,000** in organic
  sales you're *exempt* from certification (while still having to follow the
  rules), which for a first year is usually the right answer.
- **Don't say "chemical-free."** It's meaningless and it's a claim. "Grown
  without synthetic pesticides" is defensible if it's true and you can document
  it.

Growing your own is a marketing story ("estate-grown calendula") far more than
it is a cost saving. The herb is $0.34 of a $16 bottle.

## 7. The 90-day plan

**Weeks 1–2 — decide and register**
- Pick 4 products max: one salve, one tincture, one tea blend, one capsule.
- Check your state's cottage-food list for *those specific products*.
- Form the LLC ($50–$500), get the free EIN, open a business bank account.
- Quote product liability insurance — you need the number before you price.

**Weeks 3–4 — source and test**
- Order **4 oz each of 8–10 herbs** from Monterey Bay Herb Co. (no minimum).
  Total: about **$120**.
- Order one case of bottles, one case of jars, 1 gallon of 190-proof, 1,000
  labels' worth of artwork.
- Make three test batches. Write down every gram and minute. Weigh the finished
  units.

**Weeks 5–6 — label and price**
- Build the label against the rules above: identity, net quantity, Supplement
  Facts **only** if it's a supplement, your address, the disclaimer if you make
  any structure/function claim, lot code and best-by.
- Price at the *retail* column, then check it still works at half of that.
- Set wholesale minimum at **$100–$150**, prepaid, no terms.

**Weeks 7–12 — sell in one channel, twice a week**
- One farmers market, every week. $10–$45 a day.
- Bundle three products at **$35–$40** — this is what lifts the day from $250 to
  $450.
- Collect emails with a QR code; a restock email is worth more than a discount.
- After four weeks, take the numbers to 5 local shops: apothecary, gift shop,
  farm store, yoga studio, skin-care boutique. Bring samples and a one-page line
  sheet.

**Budget for the 90 days: ~$2,000–$2,900** — insurance $540, LLC $200, herbs and
bottles $450, labels/cartons $250, market fees $200, tent/table/scale $300,
samples $100, website $100.

## 8. Where this actually goes wrong

1. **Selling capsules or tinctures from an unlicensed kitchen.** It's the most
   common and it's the one that ends the business with a recall.
2. **A disease claim on the label or in an Instagram caption.** "Fights
   inflammation" is a claim; "reduces arthritis pain" is a drug claim. The
   caption counts.
3. **Skipping insurance to save $540**, then a single adverse-reaction letter
   costs more than the year's revenue.
4. **Buying 5 lb of an herb you've never made with.** Sample first.
5. **Wholesale before retail.** Selling to shops before you know which product
   reorders is how you end up with 200 unsold units and 90-day terms.
6. **Ignoring the alcohol rules.** A 40% ABV tincture may be an alcohol beverage
   to TTB regardless of what you call it.
7. **Assuming the market is the business.** Median market vendor sales are about
   $2,200 a year. The business is repeat customers, not foot traffic.
