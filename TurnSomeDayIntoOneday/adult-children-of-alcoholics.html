Adult children of alcoholics

You were the sensible one. You were nine.

You learned to read a room before you learned to ride a bike. You knew from the front door which kind of night it was going to be. And you got so good at managing it that thirty years later you're still doing it — in your job, in your marriage, with people who never drank a drop. That's the part nobody warns you about: the house ends, and the habits don't.

What the phrase means

"Adult children of alcoholics" — ACOA, or ACA — is the name for people who grew up in a house where somebody drank, and who carry the shape of that house into adult life. You'll also see "adult child syndrome," which is the same idea. It doesn't mean you're childish. It means part of you had to grow up at a speed no child should have to, and another part didn't get to grow up at all.

It is not a diagnosis. It's a description a lot of people read once and recognize immediately.

The same description fits houses where the problem wasn't alcohol: pills, gambling, rage, a parent who was mentally ill and untreated, or one who was simply never there. The drink is the common version, not the only one.

The traits people recognize

The fellowship that formed around this has a well-known list of traits — usually called the Laundry List — and it's worth reading in their own words rather than mine. What people describe, over and over, comes out roughly like this:

  • You're a fine judge of everyone's mood but your own, and you scan a room without meaning to
  • Anger and raised voices frighten you more than they should, or you've become the one who raises them
  • You feel responsible when other people are unhappy, including people whose unhappiness has nothing to do with you
  • Criticism goes straight through the armor. Praise doesn't stick at all
  • You either take charge of everything or hand the whole thing over — the middle is unfamiliar ground
  • Calm feels suspicious. Chaos feels oddly like home, so you keep finding it
  • You apologize constantly, including for things that are plainly not yours
  • You're extremely loyal, well past the point where the loyalty is deserved
  • Somewhere in there is a quiet, permanent sense that you're not quite enough

If you read that and felt slightly caught out — that reaction is the whole reason the list exists.

What actually helps

Not blame. Almost everyone arrives at this braced to be told to hate a parent, and that isn't it — plenty of people doing this work love the parent they're describing, and some are burying them. Understanding where a habit came from is not the same as sending anyone a bill.

The work is noticing which of these you're still doing in a house that is nothing like that one.

That's what this app is for, and the section built for the person alongside an addiction turns out to fit here almost exactly, because the job you were given as a child is the same job:

  • Sorting what's yours to carry from what was handed to you at nine years old
  • What to do with the alertness when there's nothing left to be alert about
  • Saying no once, out loud, and surviving it
  • A private journal nobody else reads — including the things you've never said
  • And if a habit of your own came out of that house: thirteen of them are covered, without a lecture

The rooms, if you want people

There's a fellowship for exactly this — Adult Children of Alcoholics & Dysfunctional Families, usually shortened to ACA or ACOA. Free, anonymous, in person and online, and made up entirely of people who grew up the way you did. If you want the Laundry List in full or a meeting near you, that's theirs to give and it's at adultchildren.org.

If the drinking is current rather than historic — a parent still drinking now — Al-Anon is the other room, and plenty of people go to both.

We can tell you what these are. We can't tell you where your meeting is, and we won't pretend otherwise.

A therapist is not overkill

An app is a place to stand, not the whole answer. Childhood material has a way of coming up sideways years later, and a therapist who works with family-of-origin or trauma is worth it — particularly if what surfaces is heavier than you expected. Taking it seriously isn't dramatic. It's proportionate.

If tonight is worse than that

If you're in crisis, or you've had thoughts of hurting yourself, please talk to a person tonight — not an app. Call or text 988 (US), any hour, free and confidential.

If you're not safe where you live — now, as an adult, or if a child in your life isn't — the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788.
