Is it binge eating, or did I just overeat?

Everybody overeats. Christmas exists. The difference people describe is not the amount — it is the feeling of not being able to stop once it has started, and what happens in your head afterwards.

Overeating is uncomfortable and then it is over. A binge tends to come with three other things: it is usually done alone or hidden, it is faster than normal eating, and it is followed by shame rather than just a full stomach. If you have ever eaten something and then got rid of the packaging so nobody would know, you already understand the difference.

What it usually is not

It is not greed, and it is not a discipline problem. That framing is almost universal among people who do this, and it is the single thing that keeps it going — because shame is not a brake, it is the fuel. The unpleasant feeling that follows a binge is exactly the feeling the next binge is there to switch off.

It is also not usually about hunger. Ask honestly what the twenty minutes before looked like and it is nearly always one of four things: exhausted, bored, lonely, or holding a feeling you did not want to sit in.

Why restriction makes it worse

The most common cycle people describe runs like this: a binge, then shame, then a strict plan on Monday — cutting out food groups, skipping meals, starting again properly. That plan holds for a few days, and the deprivation itself sets up the next binge, which arrives with more shame than the last one.

If every attempt to fix this has been a stricter rule, and every stricter rule has ended the same way, the rules are not failing because you are weak. They are part of the machine.

What tends to actually help

  • Eat regularly, including breakfast. Dull advice, and the one that moves this most. A body that has not eaten since morning is not making a free choice at nine at night.
  • Stop the rules that trigger the cycle. Foods you have labelled forbidden are usually the ones a binge is built from.
  • Name your window. Nearly everyone has one — a time of day and a set of conditions. Yours is probably specific and predictable, and once you can name it you can put something else in it.
  • Do not skip the next meal to compensate. That is the move that guarantees a repeat, and it feels like the responsible thing, which is what makes it so hard to give up.
  • Tell one person. Secrecy is not a side effect of this. It is load-bearing.

When to talk to somebody in person

This page is not a diagnosis and neither is the check-in above. Please talk to a doctor — not an app — if you are making yourself sick, using laxatives or diet pills, exercising to make up for eating, if your weight is changing fast in either direction, or if any of this has your day organised around it. Those are treatable and there are people who do nothing else. Going is not an overreaction.

In the US, the National Alliance for Eating Disorders helpline is 1-866-662-1235, staffed by licensed clinicians. If you are in crisis, call or text 988.
