The Book of Spells

Twenty-seven workings, and the body they work on

How to use this book

A spell is not a way of asking the world to change without you. It is a ritual that makes the next action possible — the breath before the lift, the word before the apology, the small ceremony that turns a decision into a thing you actually did.

Every working here is built from something real: breath, food, heat, sleep, movement, naming, writing, and the closing of a day. Each one tells you why it works, so that when it does work you know which part to keep.

Read this before the first page

Nothing in this book treats illness, replaces a doctor, pays a debt, or makes a person love you. A spell sits beside the medicine, the money and the conversation — it is the part that gets you to them. If a working is being used to avoid one of those, it is not a spell any more; it is a delay.

I

The rules of the working

Five rules. They are not superstition; they are what separates a ritual that changes a person from a gesture that changes nothing.

1
Name what it is for.

A spell with no stated purpose becomes a mood. Say the purpose out loud in one sentence, in the present tense, and make it small enough to be true: not "let me be wealthy" but "let me ask for the price I set."

2
Gather three things.

One for the body (water, salt, heat, breath), one for the mind (a word, a name, a number), one for the place (a candle, a stone, a cloth, a door). Three is enough to make an occasion; more is decoration.

3
Say it once, slowly, out loud.

Out loud, because a thought can be renegotiated and a sentence in the air cannot. Slowly, because the body believes a slow voice. Once, because repetition turns a working into a wish.

4
Do one thing afterwards, within the day.

This is the whole of it. The spell that is not followed by an action is a rehearsal. The action does not have to be large — it has to be the next one.

5
Close it.

Put the things away, open the door or the window, say the closing line, and go and be ordinary. A working left open is a worry left running.

The closing line

It is done, and it is enough for today.

Every spell in this book ends there. It is not a flourish; ending deliberately is what tells the body the ceremony is over and the rest of the day can begin.

II

For the body

The body is the one altar you cannot leave behind. These seven are the oldest kind of spell: breath, fuel, heat, sleep, movement, food and posture — the things that were true before anyone wrote them down.

Before effort · for the empty tank

The Spell of the Second Wind

For the moment before work you are not sure you have the strength for.

You need

A glass of water, a piece of fruit or a spoon of honey, and the first exercise written down where you can see it.

Say

What I have is what I will spend.
What I spend, I will replace.
Begin.

Do

Drink the water. Eat the fruit. Put your hand flat on your sternum and breathe out longer than you breathe in, four times. Then do the first exercise, badly, and let the second one be better.

Why it worksMuscle runs on glycogen and fluid, and the "second wind" is usually the moment fuel and blood flow catch up with demand. Carbohydrate before effort spares the protein you would otherwise burn, and a long exhale lowers the heart rate enough to make starting possible. The fruit is not a metaphor.

Any hour · for the racing chest

The Spell of the Slow Breath

For anger, panic, or the hour before something you cannot avoid.

You need

A closed door, a seat, and six minutes.

Say

In for four. Hold for two.
Out for six, and the door closes with it.
Nothing is decided in this breath.
Nothing is decided in the next.

Do

Six rounds, then one long exhale that is twice as long as the inhale — the physiological sigh. Then sit for one more minute without doing anything at all.

Why it worksA long exhale raises vagal tone and slows the heart within seconds; the sigh is the body's own reset and works even in a car. Six minutes is enough to move from the alarm to the decision, and most things done from the alarm are the things we regret.

After effort · for what hurts

The Spell of the Warm Cloth

For a sore back, a stiff shoulder, or the second day after a hard session.

You need

A cloth, hot water, and ten minutes with nothing else in your hands.

Say

Heat to the place that carried the weight.
Blood to the place that did the work.
Pain is a messenger, not a master.
I have heard it. Let it rest.

Do

Wring the cloth, lay it on, and let it cool before re-wetting: three rounds. Then move the joint gently through the range that does not hurt — heat without movement only feels good.

Why it worksHeat widens vessels and relaxes muscle guarding, which is why it helps stiffness more than fresh injury. Ice is for the first day and for swelling; heat is for the days after. Neither repairs the tissue — movement and load do that — but both make the movement possible.

At night · for the short sleep

The Spell of Nine Hours

For the person who has decided sleep is a luxury.

You need

A dark room, a cool one, and a time written down for tomorrow that you will actually keep.

Say

What is unfinished stays unfinished.
What is urgent will still be urgent at six.
I am not the only thing holding this up.
The day is closed.

Do

Write the one thing tomorrow needs on paper and leave it out of the bedroom. Dim everything. Lie down before you are exhausted, not after.

Why it worksSleep is when muscle is repaired, memory is filed and the brain clears waste. Short sleep raises cortisol and appetite and lowers insulin sensitivity — one week of it moves blood sugar in a healthy person. You cannot out-supplement it, and no spell is louder than a screen at midnight.

Every day · for the still body

The Spell of the Walking Cure

For low mood, a sluggish gut, and legs that have forgotten their job.

You need

Twenty minutes, shoes, and no destination.

Say

I do not walk to arrive.
I walk so the blood remembers its road.
Left, right, left — and the day moves with me.

Do

Walk out for ten minutes, turn, come back. Do not make it a workout. Do it again tomorrow.

Why it worksWalking pumps the veins in the legs, moves the gut, and lets muscle take sugar out of the blood without insulin. It also raises BDNF, a growth factor for the brain. Twenty minutes is the dose at which the effect starts, and it is free.

At the table · for the fast eater

The Spell of the Long Table

For eating standing up, in the car, or in six minutes.

You need

A plate, a glass of water, and a chair.

Say

This came from the ground, or from something that ate from the ground.
I will sit for it.
I will taste the first three bites.
Then I will decide about the rest.

Do

Sit. Water first. Put the fork down between bites for the first five minutes. Notice the point where the food stops tasting as good — that is the signal, not the empty plate.

Why it worksFullness signals reach the brain minutes after the stomach has had enough, so speed reliably overshoots. Sitting, water and attention slow the meal enough for the signal to arrive. Fibre and water also decide what the gut does with it afterwards, which is the whole of the next chapter of the body.

On rising · for the bent spine

The Spell of the Straight Spine

For the desk, the phone, and the shoulders that have crept forward.

You need

A wall, a doorway, and ninety seconds.

Say

Bone above bone.
Breath into the back.
The weight goes down through the heels,
and the head goes up without being lifted.

Do

Stand against the wall, heels and head touching, for one minute of breathing. Then hang from the top of a doorway for twenty seconds with the arms overhead, and let the ribs drop.

Why it worksBone responds to load and muscle responds to length; a minute of honest standing is load, and the hang is traction. Neither fixes a spine that is loaded badly all day — that is what the movement and the training are for — but both interrupt the pattern at the moment it is setting.

III

For the mind

The mind cannot be commanded, but it can be given a shape to hold. These workings use naming, writing, water and endings — the four things that reliably move a stuck head.

Before starting · for the frozen

The Spell of the Small Beginning

For the task you have been avoiding for three weeks.

You need

A timer and a sentence that begins with "the first thing is..."

Say

Not the whole thing. The first thing.
Not well. First.
Two minutes, and I may stop.

Do

Set two minutes. Do the smallest visible piece: open the file, sharpen the tool, write the first line badly. Stop when the timer ends and decide whether to continue — you almost always will.

Why it worksAvoidance is fed by the size of the imagined task, and starting is what shrinks it. The two-minute rule works because beginning is the expensive part; once begun, the task is ordinary.

When afraid · for the unnamed thing

The Spell of the Named Fear

For the dread that is worse than whatever it is about.

You need

Paper and a pen. Not a phone.

Say

I name you:                 .
You are not the whole weather.
You are one cloud with a name.
Named things can be walked around.

Do

Write the fear in one plain sentence at the top of the page. Under it write the worst version, then what you would actually do. Close the page. The fear does not vanish; it stops being shapeless.

Why it worksPutting a feeling into words lowers activity in the amygdala and raises it in the prefrontal cortex — naming is not a comfort, it is a change in which part of the brain is holding the thing. This is why a written fear is smaller than a felt one.

When asked for what you do not have

The Spell of the Closed Door

For the person who says yes before they have decided.

You need

Nothing but the pause. This one is cast in the throat.

Say

Let me look at that and come back to you.
That is not mine to carry.
No is a whole sentence.

Do

Use the first line once today, in a real conversation, and then answer within the time you said. The door is not rudeness; it is a hinge.

Why it worksMost unwanted obligations are agreed to in the two seconds before thought arrives. A rehearsed phrase buys those two seconds, and a boundary held once makes the next one cheaper.

When scattered · for the crowded head

The Spell of the Empty Page

For the morning when everything is equally urgent.

You need

One sheet, one pen, ten minutes, no screen.

Say

Everything out, nothing judged.
The page holds it so the head does not have to.
Then one line, circled, is the day.

Do

Empty the head onto the page for five minutes without stopping. Then circle exactly one thing. The rest is not abandoned; it is written down, which is where it belongs until tomorrow.

Why it worksUnfinished tasks stay active in working memory and cost attention until they are externalised. Writing them down is not organising; it is unloading. One circled line is what turns a list into a day.

In the middle of it · for the spiral

The Spell of the Cold Water

For the hour when breathing has not been enough.

You need

A sink, cold water, and a cloth.

Say

Cold to the face, and the body remembers it is a body.
The story can wait.
I am here, and the room is real.

Do

Hold the cold cloth over the face for fifteen to thirty seconds, or splash cold water. Then name five things you can see and three you can hear. Repeat once if needed.

Why it worksCold water on the face triggers the dive reflex: heart rate drops, blood is redirected to the brain, and the parasympathetic system is switched on hard. It is a physical interrupt, and it is why it works when reasoning does not.

At the end of the day · for the unclosed

The Spell of the Unfinished Day

For the person who cannot stop working in their head.

You need

Three minutes and a pen.

Say

This is what was done. This is what was not.
This is the first thing tomorrow.
The rest is tomorrow's, not tonight's.

Do

Write three lines: done, not done, first thing tomorrow. Close the book. Say the closing line and physically leave the desk or the room.

Why it worksA shutdown ritual reduces the intrusive thoughts that follow work home, because the brain stops rehearsing what it believes will be forgotten. The paper is the memory, so the mind can stop being one.

IV

For the heart

These are the workings people actually need at three in the morning. None of them works on another person's will — they work on what you are still carrying.

For the thing unsaid

The Spell of the Unsaid Letter

For the conversation you cannot have, or the person who will not hear it.

You need

Paper, pen, and a safe way to burn or keep what you write.

Say

I write what I did not say.
I do not send it to change you.
I write it to stop carrying it in my hands.

Do

Write the letter without stopping, including the parts that are unfair. Read it once, aloud. Then choose: send it, keep it, or burn it in a safe place. All three are endings; only one is a message.

Why it worksExpressive writing about what is unresolved lowers stress reactivity and improves mood across many studies, and the mechanism is the same as naming a fear: the material moves from felt to formed. The letter is not for them. It is for the place in you that has been holding it.

For a death · for a name

The Spell of the Returned Name

For grief that has nowhere to go on an ordinary Tuesday.

You need

A photograph or an object, a candle, and a few minutes alone.

Say

I say your name, and the room is bigger for it.
I do not call you back.
I keep what you gave me, and I put the rest down.
Your name, and then my own.

Do

Light the candle. Say the name out loud. Say one thing you are grateful for and one thing you are angry about, because grief is both. Let the candle burn down or blow it out deliberately, and then do something ordinary with your hands.

Why it worksRitual gives grief a container and a time, which is what stops it leaking into every hour. Naming the dead is how a relationship is continued rather than ended, and saying the anger as well as the love is what keeps the ritual honest.

For a bond · for two people

The Spell of the Shared Cup

For a friendship, a partnership, or a house that needs re-making.

You need

One drink, two cups, and both people present and willing. This is never cast on someone who has not agreed.

Say

One thing poured into two.
Not the same, but from the same.
What I have, I share. What I need, I say.

Do

Pour one drink into two cups. Each person says one thing they will bring and one thing they need. Drink. The words are the spell; the drink is only the clock.

Why it worksShared food and drink are among the oldest signals of safety humans have, and a stated exchange makes an abstract bond into a concrete agreement. Both people saying a need is the part that prevents the ritual from becoming one person's performance.

For letting go

The Spell of the Cold Hearth

For the person you keep reopening the door for.

You need

Something small that belonged to the hope, and a bin or a fire.

Say

I keep the lesson and put down the hope.
The door is closed, and it is allowed to stay closed.
I do not need you to understand it.

Do

Hold the object. Say the line. Put it in the bin, or burn it safely, or give it away. Then close a real door in the house and lock it, once, deliberately.

Why it worksLetting go fails when it stays abstract; the body needs a physical act with a before and an after. A closed door, a locked lock and an object gone are three pieces of evidence that something has ended — and evidence is what the heart argues with.

For loneliness

The Spell of the Second Chair

For the evening when there is no one to call, or no one you will call.

You need

Two chairs, and the willingness to look foolish for five minutes.

Say

I sit in my own chair, and I speak plainly.
I sit in the other, and I answer kindly.
I am company that can be kept.

Do

Sit in the first chair and say the true thing you would tell a friend. Move to the second and answer as the friend would — kindly, and without solving it. Then go back and sit down.

Why it worksSelf-distancing — speaking to yourself in the second person, or from another chair — lowers emotional reactivity and makes hard thoughts easier to hold. It is also a rehearsal for the call you might actually make tomorrow, which is the action this spell is for.

V

For the house and the road

The oldest spells are about thresholds: the door, the fire, the road out and the road home. They work because a place you have marked is a place you pay attention to, and attention is most of safety.

At a new door · for a beginning

The Spell of the Salted Threshold

For the first night in a new place, or the first morning of a new arrangement.

You need

A pinch of salt, water, and the front door.

Say

Salt for what is kept.
Water for what is washed away.
This is a door, and I decide what crosses it.

Do

Put the salt in a saucer by the door, or a pinch on the step. Wash the handle. Then walk in and out once, consciously, and say what the place is for.

Why it worksA threshold marked is a threshold noticed, and noticing a door is the beginning of home security, of hospitality and of boundary. Salt and water are old because they are the two things that keep a house fit to live in.

At dusk · for the return

The Spell of the Lit Window

For coming home in the dark, or for making a house that waits for someone.

You need

One lamp, left on deliberately.

Say

One light for the one who is coming.
One light for the one who is already here.
The house is not empty. It is waiting.

Do

Leave one low lamp on before you go out, or light it as the first act of coming in. Do not use the overhead light for the first ten minutes home.

Why it worksWarm, low light at the end of a day signals safety and lets the nervous system come down; bright overhead light at night does the opposite and pushes sleep later. The ritual is the reminder; the light is the mechanism.

Before a journey

The Spell of the Safe Return

For a drive, a flight, or a road you do not know.

You need

The keys in your hand, and thirty seconds before the engine.

Say

Out by this road, and back by the same.
I will not hurry what cannot be hurried.
Let me be the one who arrives.

Do

Check the tyres, the fuel and the phone, then say it with your hand on the door. Put the phone out of reach before you move.

Why it worksMost journey disasters are decided before the journey: a bald tyre, no fuel, a phone in the hand. The spell is a checklist with a pulse, and saying it aloud is what makes it a habit rather than a good intention.

For a household · for peace

The Spell of the Unbroken Circle

For a house where the same argument keeps returning.

You need

Everyone who lives there, once, at the same table.

Say

We are one circle, and the circle is not a weapon.
What is said here stays here.
What is decided here is kept here.
And what is broken here is mended here.

Do

Sit together. Each person says one thing that must stop and one thing that must continue. Write both lists. Agree the date you will read them again, and put it where everyone can see it.

Why it worksRecurring conflict is usually a missing agreement, not a missing feeling. A stated, mutual, written agreement changes what people can appeal to in the middle of an argument — and a review date is what keeps it from becoming a plaque on the wall.

For a cluttered room

The Spell of the Cleared Step

For the room that has become a storage unit for decisions.

You need

One bag, one bin, and a single surface.

Say

One surface clear, and the room remembers its shape.
What I have not used, I do not owe.
Out, or away, or here — but decided.

Do

Clear one surface completely — the table, the step, the chair. Everything on it goes to the bin, the bag, or its place. Then stop, even if the room is not finished.

Why it worksClutter is deferred decisions, and deferred decisions are a low, constant cognitive load. One cleared surface gives the room a fixed point to return to, and stopping on purpose is what makes it repeatable rather than a purge you will not do again.

VI

For the work and the coin

Money is not a spiritual matter until it is, and then it is almost always about being seen asking. These four are for makers, sellers and anyone who has to name a price out loud.

At the beginning · for the first sale

The Spell of the First Coin

For the day the work becomes a thing that is sold.

You need

The first coin or note you are paid, and a place to keep it that is not your pocket.

Say

This is the first, and it is not the last.
I made a thing, and someone chose it.
I will do it again tomorrow.

Do

Do not spend the first coin. Put it somewhere you will see it, and write the date and what was sold on a slip of paper beside it. The next one is spent.

Why it worksThe first sale is evidence against the story that no one would want it, and evidence is what confidence is actually made of. Keeping the coin makes the evidence permanent, which matters on the days the story comes back.

Before selling · for the flinch

The Spell of the Buyer's Eye

For the table, the stall, the listing, the message you have not sent.

You need

Your price written down where you can see it, and three sentences about what the thing does.

Say

I am not asking to be liked. I am offering a thing.
The price is the price, and it is not an apology.
They are allowed to say no.

Do

Say the price out loud three times before you go. Write the three sentences. When someone asks, answer the question they asked, and let the silence after the price sit for two seconds without filling it.

Why it worksMost discounting happens in the pause after the price, from the seller's discomfort rather than the buyer's objection. Rehearsing the number and tolerating two seconds of silence is the whole skill, and it is worth more than any marketing.

At the bench · for the craft

The Spell of the Steady Hand

For the work that has to be done well and cannot be rushed.

You need

A clear surface, the right tool, and one thing less than you think you need.

Say

Slow is smooth, and smooth is fast.
One thing at a time, and the hand will learn it.
I am not late. I am accurate.

Do

Clear the surface. Do the first unit completely before starting the second. Put the phone in another room, not face-down on the bench.

Why it worksTask-switching costs time and errors, and the phone on the bench costs attention even when it is silent. One unit at a time is not a virtue; it is the cheapest quality control there is.

When the invoice is due

The Spell of the Paid Price

For asking to be paid without apologising for it.

You need

The number, the date, and one sentence.

Say

The work is done. The price was agreed.
I am not asking a favour; I am keeping an agreement.
And I will ask again on the date.

Do

Send the invoice with the date it was agreed. If it is late, ask once in plain words, without the story about why you need it. Then diarise the next ask before you close the page.

Why it worksBeing paid is a boundary, not an emotion, and a plain request is harder to refuse than a long explanation. The diarised follow-up is what turns one ask into a system — which is what a business is.

VIII

The words that are never said

A book of spells that has no prohibitions is a book of wishes. These are the lines this book will not cross, written down so that a bad hour cannot talk you past them.

Never on another person's will

No spell to make someone love you, return to you, forgive you, hire you or want you. The moment a working depends on overriding another person's choice, it is not a spell; it is a plan to take something. The shared cup is cast with both people present, or not at all.

Never to harm

No working whose purpose is another person's illness, failure, loss or fear. Not because the universe will punish it — because you will have practised being the kind of person who does that, and that is a cost you pay whether or not the spell works.

Never instead of medicine

No spell for a lump, a bleed, a chest pain, a fever that will not go, or a child who is unwell. Those are the doctor's, and the spell is what gets you to the appointment on time. A working used instead of treatment is not faith; it is delay with a candle on it.

Never for money you did not earn

No spell for a windfall that requires someone else to lose. The work and the coin chapter is about asking to be paid for a thing you made. That is the whole of the magic in that direction.

What to do when someone asks for a curse

Ask what was taken. Almost always the request is for dignity, not revenge, and dignity can be worked for directly: a letter, a boundary, a witness, a door that stays closed. Offer that instead. If they insist, say plainly that this book does not do it, and stay in the room — the refusal is the kindness.

IX

Your own workings

The book is not finished until it has your handwriting in it. Write a working of your own — a purpose, the words, and the one action that follows. It is kept on this phone only, and it is never sent anywhere.

The whole book in four lines

Name it. One sentence, present tense, small enough to be true.

Gather three things. One for the body, one for the mind, one for the place.

Say it once, out loud, slowly. Then close it: it is done, and it is enough for today.

Do the next thing within the day. That is the spell. Everything else is the ceremony around it.
