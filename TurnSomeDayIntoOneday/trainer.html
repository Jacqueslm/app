The programme below is a workout, and two lines outrank it.

A doctor's rule wins over this page, always. If a doctor has said no to a lift or a movement, that is the answer here, and nothing on this page changes it.

If there is a heart condition, blood pressure, a pregnancy, a recent operation or a back that has gone recently, ask a doctor before starting anything — and if any of it is already being looked at or medicated, that stays exactly as it is.

And stop, on the day, if there is chest pain, or dizziness, or something sharp rather than a muscle working. That is not pushing on.

The by-10 programme

A rotation of moves. Each one for a set of about ten repetitions, then ten seconds of rest, then the next move. When the list runs out, the round starts again.

The ten seconds is the whole idea and it is deliberate: long enough to breathe and set up the next move, short enough that the heart rate stays up and the session is over quickly. It is not a rest to shorten.

The week is three lifting days, two cardio days and one stretch day, repeated. A rotation is one pass through a day's list of moves.

The two levels

Two and only two. Start at the beginner level, and step up when the beginner level has been completed comfortably rather than survived — a rush at the step gives four weeks of progress and a month of tendon pain.

Beginner: three rounds, ten repetitions, eight rotations, a thirty-second last move, twenty minutes of cardio. Advanced: five rounds, twelve repetitions, ten rotations, a sixty-second last move, thirty minutes of cardio.

The three lifting days

Eight moves to a round, straight through, ten seconds between each. Work of the same kind is kept apart on purpose — a squat, then a push, then a pull — which is what makes ten seconds enough for the muscle that just worked.

  2. Push-up
  3. Dumbbell bent-over row
  4. Dumbbell Romanian deadlift
  5. Dumbbell shoulder press
  6. Dumbbell reverse lunge
  7. Plank
  8. Dumbbell curl

  1. Dumbbell sumo squat
  2. Pull-up
  3. Dumbbell floor press
  4. Dumbbell one-arm row
  5. Dumbbell lateral raise
  6. Glute bridge
  7. Hanging knee raise
  8. Dumbbell overhead triceps extension

Day C — legs and core

  1. Dumbbell split squat
  2. Dumbbell calf raise
  3. Deep push-up
  4. Dumbbell renegade row
  5. Dumbbell front raise
  6. Hip thrust
  7. Dead bug
  8. Mountain climber

The last move of a rotation is usually the timed hold — thirty seconds at the beginner level, sixty at the advanced. The pull-up comes early on B, while the grip is fresh, and the hanging knee raise comes late, because it is the same grip under load. A move that is failing is ended, not rescued; the ten-second rest is what makes ending a set early possible.

The two cardio days

One slot, five choices: plyometrics, jump rope, kickboxing, sweat step and dancing. Four of the five run on minutes alone — twenty at the beginner level, thirty at the advanced, or anywhere from twenty to forty by preference.

They are alternatives and not a ladder. Sweat step is the low-impact one; the adaptation does not care which of the five it came from, and the one still being done in six months is the one that works.

Only plyometrics uses the rotation, because jumping is a skill that improves with short, repeatable, high-quality efforts: four moves, three of them for twenty seconds and the last for thirty seconds to a minute, then round again. Landing is the safety: land soft, hips back, knees tracking, never on a locked leg.

The stretch day

The sixth day is the stretch day: twelve movements held for thirty seconds each, one round, no rest timer and no load — a chest opener, cat-cow, child's pose, downward dog, a hip flexor stretch, a figure-4 glute stretch, a seated hamstring stretch, a quad stretch, a calf stretch, thread the needle, a supine twist, and neck and shoulder rolls.

The rules are the opposite of the lifting days: hold to tension rather than to pain, breathe out into it, enter and leave the range slowly. Thirty seconds matters because the first ten are the body deciding whether to allow it at all.

It is a session in the programme rather than a rest from it, it is the day most often skipped, and it is not the same thing as a warm-up — the warm-up is five to eight minutes before a lifting day, and it is the last thing to cut when time is short.

What it needs, and how it gets harder

The kit is a pair of dumbbells, push-up bars, a pull-up bar, and floor space. Nothing in it assumes a machine, and the moves are drawn from what those four things can do. Where the kit is short, the pattern is regressed rather than dropped — a push-up on a bench, a pull-up as a hanging hold, a row with a lighter dumbbell and a shorter range.

It gets harder in one order: the movement gets harder first — a push-up becomes a deep push-up — then the repetitions, then the rounds, then the load. The rest stays at ten seconds throughout, because the ten seconds is the programme's identity rather than a variable.

And the pair that keeps it honest: load is added when the movement is good for the whole set at the current one, not when the last rep felt hard. Training to failure on every set is a choice, and it is not this programme's choice.

The P90X rotation

The by-10 above is built on P90X. This is that programme's classic ninety-day rotation: thirteen weeks in three phases, with a recovery week at the end of each phase, and six days on and one off every week.

Phase 1 (weeks 1–3) and Phase 2 (weeks 5–7) each use one resistance block: Chest & Back, Plyometrics, Shoulders & Arms, Yoga X, Legs & Back, Kenpo X; then Chest, Shoulders & Triceps, Plyometrics, Back & Biceps, Yoga X, Legs & Back, Kenpo X. Phase 3 (weeks 9–12) alternates the two week by week — weeks 9 and 11 are the Chest & Back block, weeks 10 and 12 the Chest, Shoulders & Triceps block. The five resistance days finish with Ab Ripper X, sixteen minutes.

Weeks 4, 8 and 13 are recovery weeks, and they are the same shape but no load: Yoga X, Core Synergistics, Kenpo X, X Stretch, Core Synergistics, Yoga X, then rest. That week is the programme, not a week off — it is where the joints and the sleep catch up with the training, and the last one closes the ninety days.
