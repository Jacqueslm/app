How do I know if he's an alcoholic, or just drinks a lot?

Honestly? That question is a trap, and it is the one that keeps women stuck for years. There is no line he crosses that makes it official, no number of drinks that settles it, and no label you can hold up that will make him agree with you.

A better question: is his drinking costing you things?

Sleep. Money. Evenings you planned. Conversations that go sideways after nine o'clock. A version of yourself that used to be easier to be around. Those are facts about your life, and you do not need his agreement or a doctor's word to count them.

Why "I only had two" is so hard to argue with

Because you are not actually arguing about the number. You are arguing about whether you are allowed to trust what you saw. That is why these conversations leave you feeling unhinged rather than heard — you came in with a concern and left defending your own memory.

The way out is not a better argument. It is to stop negotiating the facts and start being specific about your own line: not "you drink too much", which he can dispute forever, but "I'm not doing Friday nights like that anymore", which he cannot.

Telling a promise from a change

Sunday promises are real when he makes them. That is what makes them so confusing. Here is what people who actually stop tend to do, and what a promise on its own never does:

  • They change something structural — who they see, where they go, what is in the house — rather than just resolving harder.
  • They tell somebody other than you. Secrecy is the fuel; one other person knowing changes the odds more than any amount of willpower.
  • They have a plan for the specific hour it usually happens, decided in advance rather than invented in the moment.
  • A bad day does not become a bad fortnight, because there is a plan for that too.
  • They stop asking you to manage it for them.

None of that requires him to use the word alcoholic. It is just the difference between meaning it and doing it, and after a while you can see it from across the room.

What actually helps, and what quietly doesn't

Doesn't: counting for him, pouring it away, checking receipts, timing his return, or having the conversation at eleven at night when he has been drinking. Nobody has ever been reasoned out of it at eleven.

Does: saying the true thing once, in daylight, plainly. Telling one person you trust so you are not the only one who knows. Putting back one thing of your own — a friendship, an evening, an appointment you keep moving. And deciding in advance what you will do next time, so the decision is not made in the middle of it.

You are allowed to want your own life back

Not instead of him. Alongside. The idea that you have to solve him first, and then you get to have a life, is the exact belief that keeps this going for a decade — and it is not true, and it never was.

If tonight is worse than that

If you're in crisis, or you've had thoughts of hurting yourself, please talk to a person tonight — not an app. Call or text 988 (US), any hour, free and confidential.

If you're afraid of him — not just hurt, afraid — the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. You don't have to be in danger tonight to call. You're allowed to call just to ask whether what you're living with is normal.
