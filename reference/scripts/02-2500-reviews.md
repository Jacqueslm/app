# I read 2,500 reviews of sobriety apps

**~12 min · unrestricted · film this one first**

Every number in this script is real and comes from the scrape in
`scraper-output/reviews-top25.csv`. Do not round them up on camera. If you
change a number, change it here too.

- 2,500 reviews
- 25 apps
- 581 of those reviews are one or two star
- 165 mention price, paywalls, subscriptions or refunds — that's 7%
- of those 165, **66% are one or two star**
- "screen time" is the single most common two-word phrase — 50 times
- 6 of the 25 apps are screen-time apps

## COLD OPEN — no intro, straight in

I read two and a half thousand reviews of sobriety apps.

Not the five-star ones the developers screenshot. All of them. Every one I
could get.

And there's a pattern in there that I don't think anybody's said out loud, so
I'm going to say it.

## WHAT I ACTUALLY DID

Twenty-five apps. Sobriety apps, quit-smoking apps, screen time apps, a couple
of mental health ones. A hundred reviews each. Two and a half thousand in
total.

I'm not a data scientist. I counted words. That's it. I wanted to know what
people actually write when they've used one of these things for a few months —
not what the app store page says, what the person says afterwards.

I should say up front: I make one of these apps. So you can take everything
after this with that in mind. But I didn't pick the reviews and I didn't pick
the apps, and I'll show you the ones that made me look bad too.

## FINDING ONE — the word nobody expects

The most common thing people write about, across all two and a half thousand
reviews, is **screen time**.

Fifty mentions. More than craving. More than relapse. More than sober.

And when I looked at which apps were in there, six of the twenty-five were
screen time apps. Not quit-drinking apps. Screen time.

Here's why I think that matters. Nobody sits down and says "I'm addicted to my
phone." That sentence sounds ridiculous, so nobody says it. What they say is
"my screen time is bad."

It's the same behaviour. It's the same loop — the hand goes before the decision
does, you do it longer than you meant to, you feel worse after. But it's got a
polite name, so people will actually go looking for help with it.

That's not a small thing. That's a whole category of people who'd never type
the word addiction into anything, quietly looking for the same help.

## FINDING TWO — where people actually get angry

Now the part I didn't expect.

A hundred and sixty-five of those reviews mention price. Paywalls,
subscriptions, free trials, refunds.

And two thirds of them are one or two stars.

Read that again. When somebody mentions the price of one of these apps, two
times out of three, they're furious.

The exact phrases that come up, over and over: "pay wall". "free version".
"cancel subscription". "free trial".

## FINDING THREE — the moment it breaks

Here's the shape of it, and this is the bit that got me.

People don't quit these apps because the app is bad. Loads of the angry reviews
say the app is good. They say "this was great and then."

They quit at the card.

They download something on a bad night. Which — think about when somebody
downloads a sobriety app. It's not a Tuesday afternoon. It's late, it's after
something happened, they've decided tonight's the night. They put in day one.
They come back the next day. They come back the day after.

And then somewhere around day eight, day ten, they go to open the thing they've
been building — their own count, their own days, the one bit of evidence that
they did something — and there's a wall in front of it. Sixty dollars a year.

That's the moment. That's where the one-star reviews come from.

And I want to be careful here, because I'm not saying those developers are
bad people. Running an app costs money. Everybody's got to eat. I'm not
pretending otherwise.

But I think there's a difference between charging for extra things and
charging for the thing the person did. Your streak isn't a feature. You made
that. You made it on nights nobody saw.

## WHAT I DID WITH THAT

So when I built mine, I made a rule about it.

The day counter is free. The panic button — the thing you press when it's
eleven at night and it's coming — that's free. The breathing, the grounding,
the voice that talks you through it when you can't read, free. The journal,
typed or spoken, free. The first fifteen days of every programme, free.

No card. Not a trial. Free.

There is a paid version, and I'll tell you exactly what's in it so nobody finds
out later at a bad moment: days sixteen to ninety of the longer programmes, the
AI companion, and the live rooms. That's it. That's the whole list.

If you never pay me a penny, the counter still counts and the panic button
still works. That was the point.

## WHAT I GOT WRONG

I said I'd tell you what made me look bad, so here it is.

I put a video up eleven days ago. Fifteen sessions, all in one, and I called it
"Days one to fifteen, full guided sessions."

Seven views.

And when I went and looked at my own research — the same research I've just
shown you — the answer was sitting right there. Nobody searches for "full
guided sessions." That's what I'd call it if I made it. It's not what you'd
call it if you needed it.

You'd type a question. You'd type "why do I do it when I don't even want to."

So that's on me, and I'm fixing it, and I'm telling you because I'd rather do
that than pretend the last year has been a straight line.

## CLOSE

Two and a half thousand reviews. Three things in them.

People will look for help with the polite version of a problem before they'll
look for help with the real name of it.

They don't leave because the app failed. They leave at the card.

And the thing they built — the days — that was never the app's to sell back to
them.

It's free. It's turnsomedayintodayone.com. There's no card and there's nothing
to cancel.
