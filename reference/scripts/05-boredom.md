# Nobody warns you about the boredom

**~7 min · unrestricted · the most distinctive one here**

Already written into the app on tower floors 31–40 and lesson day 33. Almost
nobody in the category talks about this, which is exactly why it travels.

---

## COLD OPEN

Everybody warns you about the cravings.

Nobody warns you about the Tuesday.

Nothing's wrong on the Tuesday. You're not white-knuckling anything. You're
just sat there at half seven in the evening with four hours in front of you
and absolutely nothing to put in them.

That's the one that gets people. Not the dramatic night. The empty one.

## THE HOURS COME BACK AND THEY'RE NOT A GIFT

Whatever you stopped, it was taking up time. Real hours. Most people
underestimate how many until they stop and the hours come back all at once.

And you'd think that's the reward. That's what you were promised — you get
your evenings back.

Except an empty evening, when you're not used to one, doesn't feel like
freedom. It feels like a room with nothing in it. It's actually a bit
threatening. And you sit in it going: is this it? Is this what I traded for?

That's a normal thought. Everybody has it. Nobody says it out loud because it
sounds ungrateful.

## THE HABIT HAD A JOB

Here's the bit that actually helps.

The thing you stopped was doing a job. It filled the time and it changed how
the evening felt. Two jobs, really.

You've now removed it. The jobs are still there. Nothing's doing them.

So the gap isn't a sign you've made a mistake, and it isn't a sign you're not
committed. It's a gap where a job used to get done, and gaps get filled by
whatever's nearest. Which is why so many people stop one thing and quietly
pick up another — the phone, the food, the online shopping at midnight, the
second job they didn't need.

Not weakness. Nearest thing to hand.

## BOREDOM IS ONE OF THE BIG ONES

Boredom gets treated as a joke in recovery. It's on the lists somewhere near
the bottom, after the serious ones.

I think it's one of the big ones, and I think it's near the top for people
somewhere between week three and month three — which is exactly when everyone
else has stopped asking how you are, because from the outside it looks
finished.

The crisis is over. Everyone's relieved. And you're sat in a quiet room on a
Tuesday thinking something's wrong with you.

Nothing's wrong with you. You're bored, and you've got nothing built yet.

## YOU WILL FILL IT BADLY FIRST

This is the part I want to be honest about because I got it wrong for months.

You don't go from an empty evening to a good one. There's a stage in between
where you fill it badly. Biscuits and the phone and arguing with strangers on
the internet. That's not failure — that's the normal order. Everyone does the
bad version first.

Then it gets slightly less bad. Then one evening you notice you did something
you'd actually choose.

For me that took about five months. Five months before an empty evening
stopped feeling like a threat and started just being an evening. That sounds
like a long time. It isn't, really — but nobody told me the number, so I spent
three of those months certain I'd broken something permanently.

## WHAT ACTUALLY GOES IN THE GAP

Small and boring beats big and inspiring, every single time.

Not "I'm going to learn the guitar." Five minutes of something, badly, at a
fixed time. Something with your hands is better than something with your
head, especially early. Something outside your house is better than something
in it, because your house is where all the old cues live.

And put it in the diary like it's an appointment, because "I'll do it when I
feel like it" means it happens on the evenings you don't need it.

## CLOSE

Nobody warns you that stopping hands your evenings back and that this is the
hard part.

It's a gap where a job used to be. Something's going in it. You get a say in
what, but only if you know that's what's happening.

So: it's not a craving. You're not doing it wrong. You're bored, at the stage
where everyone stops asking, and it passes — slower than you'd like and faster
than five months feels.
